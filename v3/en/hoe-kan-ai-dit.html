How can AI do this itself? · ± 5 min · basic concepts explained

How can AI work independently for a whole night?

No prior knowledge of a VM, orchestrator, Hermes, Git or APIs needed. The starting point is simple: give a system a task with clear
acceptance criteriaAcceptance criteria are the concrete, measurable requirements a solution must meet before it counts as ‘done’ — for example: “the demo loads without errors” or “the test passes”.
, and let the system plan, build, test and improve on its own until those requirements are met.

The loop: plan, build, test, review, fix, record

Instead of estimating one big task, each challenge was cut into small pieces. For every piece, the same fixed loop repeated itself:

  1. 1PlanChoosing one small, manageable piece of work from the assignment.
  2. 2BuildActually carrying out that one piece: writing or changing code.
  3. 3TestAutomatically checking whether the result actually works, with objective proof instead of a promise.
  4. 4Independent reviewA second, separate AI assesses the actual result against the acceptance criteria.
  5. 5FixNot good enough? Back to step 2, until it's right.
  6. 6RecordAn approved step is saved and the loop starts again, with a new piece of work.

↩ Not good enough at step 4? Then back to step 2 — until the acceptance criteria are met. After that: the next piece of work, starting again from step 1.

These six steps repeated, piece by piece, until all five challenges were done. One of the five, Electron Dose Lab, is not a clinical device, but a genuine simulation based on public NIST data. Want to see the full technical version of this loop, with the exact roles and state transitions? That's on Under the hood.

Why an independent check matters

An AI that judges its own work misses things just as easily as a person proofreading their own text: you mostly see what you meant to make, not necessarily what's actually there. That's why a second, separate AI independently checked every result: not based on what the building AI claimed, but based on the actual result — the actual code, the actual test evidence — measured against the acceptance criteria agreed in advance. Only once that check passed could the next step begin. If not, the piece went back to the build step.

What exactly did ‘the whole night’ and ‘autonomous’ mean here?

Autonomous here doesn't mean nobody set the boundaries. Gert defined in advance what the challenges were about and when a result was good enough. After that, the system repeated the loop above independently, hour after hour, for each of the five challenges: planning, building, testing, having it checked, repairing where needed, and recording what was good. A human didn't need to be present at every step or give approval — only for setting the goal and the boundaries in advance, and for judging the end result the next morning. That's the core of ‘autonomous’ in this project: the work shifted from writing code to formulating the assignment, the boundaries and what the result needed to meet.

This is what it's technically called (optional — not needed to understand the rest of this story):

orchestratorThe orchestrator is the traffic controller: the program that splits the work into small steps, has each step tested and checked, and only moves on once a step is genuinely good.
workerThe worker is the AI that actually carries out one small piece of work: writing code, making changes, or setting up tests.
reviewerThe reviewer is the second, independent AI that checks the result against the acceptance criteria, separate from the AI that built it.
HermesHermes is the toolbox: the connection that lets the AI reach tools and models, a bridge between the orchestrator and the programs that do the actual work.
GitGit is the logbook: a system that saves every change to the code, so you can always read back exactly what was changed and when.
modelThe model is the underlying AI system that reasons and writes text or code — here mainly Claude models, driven from the orchestrator.
