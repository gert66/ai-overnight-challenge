From a conversation to a working parking app

This is the conversation behind the bonus challenge for Wietse. It shows how an everyday problem, simply described out loud, turned step by step into a concrete mobile app.

About this transcript. The substance and phrasing stay as close as possible to the original conversation. Slips of the tongue, duplicate speech-recognition sentences, and non-substantive messages about credits, wait times and technical approvals have been removed. Successive corrections about the same design choice have been merged where needed.
Gert

I have a colleague, Wietse, a radiotherapist-oncologist. He told me about a problem in his neighbourhood. There are fixed parking spots, but of course they're not always used. People are home, away, on holiday or at work. The main problem is that nobody really knows which spot is free when. They try to arrange something among themselves, but that doesn't always work.

Couldn't we build some kind of app for that? Someone has their own parking spot and obviously keeps the right to it. But if they're away, someone else could use it temporarily. And if you have visitors, you'd want to simply be able to ask: can my visitor park somewhere?

ChatGPT

Yes. Then I wouldn't treat it as just a booking app, but as a system that deals with uncertainty. The owner always keeps priority on their own spot. Residents can explicitly indicate when their spot is free, but the system can also learn from recurring patterns.

The user could, for example, see immediately: "3 spots guaranteed available", "probably 4 more available" and the expected parking pressure. That way you distinguish between what's known for certain and what's likely.

Gert

Right, because the real problem is of course that people don't always fill that in properly. You can ask everyone to indicate when they're home, but that will never happen one hundred percent. Couldn't you do something probabilistic with that? So that it learns from history: this person is usually away on Tuesdays, or usually not home at this time.

And it should also be able to work with real data, but for the demo I want mock data so you can immediately show how it works.

ChatGPT

That fits well. Per spot we can use three levels of information: explicitly reported availability, recurring patterns from history, and unknown situations. An explicit report gets near-full certainty. Historical patterns yield a probability, for example 87% likely free. For residents who report little, the uncertainty stays visible.

Internally the model could compute something like P(free | resident, weekday, time, history, explicit report), weighting recent information more heavily. It doesn't need to be a heavy AI model for that. An explainable statistical model is actually attractive here.

Gert

Good. And visitors matter. So say I live there and I want to have someone over tonight. Then I want to simply be able to ask if there's a spot. And if there's more demand than supply, you need to do something smart about that too. Waiting list, fair distribution, that kind of thing.

ChatGPT

Then we make "I need a spot", "My spot is free" and "Visitor spot" the main actions. Under scarcity there can be a waiting list and a fair allocator that accounts for owner priority, duration, prior use and available shared capacity.

The scenario where an owner comes home earlier also matters. With one action, "I'm back", the owner gets their spot back and the system tries to automatically move the temporary user to another spot.

Gert

Yes. And I don't just want you to build a technical thing with tables. It really has to look good on a phone. Just something you could show Wietse and think: you'd actually use this.

And make a few preset scenarios, A, B, C, D or so. That way I don't have to enter all the data myself first to show what happens.

ChatGPT

Then it becomes mobile-first. The home screen shows current parking pressure, confirmed versus likely free, a simple forecast and large, thumb-friendly actions. Alongside that, a visual parking map where you can tap a spot for its status and the reasoning behind the prediction.

For demo mode we can simulate different types of residents, such as a reliable reporter, commuter, remote worker, shift worker and someone who never fills anything in. Scenarios A–D could show, for example, a quiet weekday, a busy evening, poor reporting discipline and lots of visitors.

Gert

It ultimately needs to be genuinely configurable. So not just a pretty picture. I want to be able to add and edit residents, link a fixed parking spot, set patterns, and add or change parking spots. The number of residents and parking spots actually has to do something.

ChatGPT

Then it needs a real management layer: adding, editing and deactivating residents; assigning fixed spots; setting weekly patterns and reliability; adding, editing or marking parking spots as visitor/shared spots. The scenarios and historical demo data remain available alongside that.

Gert

And it needs to work well on the phone. And actually both Dutch and English, with a small NL/EN switch. The demo doesn't need to be called Mara. Make Wietse the user. Not some big "Good afternoon" greeting, just compact. "Hi Wietse" and in Dutch "Hoi Wietse".

ChatGPT

Sure. The full interface becomes Dutch and English, with a compact language switch and a remembered language choice. Wietse becomes the current demo resident everywhere. The mobile view is checked at 360–430 pixels wide, without horizontal overflow and with large primary actions.

Gert

And then I eventually want to be able to publish it under WhoFirst too. So not something where people see a Lovable address. Something like parking.whofirst.nl seems good to me.

ChatGPT

That's a logical end state: the app itself stays generic and clean, without visible build-platform branding, and the public entry point becomes parking.whofirst.nl.

Result. This conversation produced a working, mobile demonstrator with an NL/EN interface, Wietse as the demo resident, resident and parking-spot management, historical mock data, probabilistic availability, visitor requests, a waiting list, owner priority, reallocation, and four demo scenarios.
